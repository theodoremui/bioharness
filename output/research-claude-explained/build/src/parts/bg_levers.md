### Four ways to improve an agent {#bg-levers}

An agent can be improved by changing any of its three parts: the **prompt** (the written instructions and tool descriptions), the **harness code** or the **model weights** ({fig:levers}). A model whose weights are left unchanged is called **frozen**.

@fig levers | figures/fig_levers.svg | What each of the four improvement methods changes, and roughly what it costs.

**Prompt optimization.** GEPA [@agrawal2025] runs the agent on training examples, hands the transcripts of failed runs to a second language model and asks it to rewrite the instructions so those failures are avoided. Rewrites that score better are kept and refined further. No weights change, so the cost is the agent's runs plus the second model's rewriting, with no training.

**Harness search.** Meta-Harness [@lee2026] applies the same idea to the harness program. A proposer model edits the code (for instance, how often to retry a failed tool, which default settings to use, or which sanity checks to run on tool outputs), the new harness is run, and the code, transcripts and score of every attempt are stored in a folder that the proposer consults before its next edit ({fig:metaharness}).

@fig metaharness | figures/papers/meta_harness_fig2.png | Harness search. A proposer writes new harness code, the harness and a frozen model are evaluated on tasks, and the code, transcripts and score of every attempt are stored for the next proposal. Reproduced from @{lee2026}, Fig. 2.

**Supervised fine-tuning (SFT).** Show the model example trajectories and adjust its weights so that, given the same inputs, it becomes more likely to produce the same outputs. SFT learns only from fixed demonstrations—it never tries an attempt of its own and finds out how well it scored. Changing all 8 billion weights is expensive, so we use **LoRA**, short for low-rank adaptation [@hu2021]. LoRA keeps the original weights frozen and learns a small correction for each large table of weights (a matrix). A table of 4,096 × 4,096 numbers, about 16.8 million, receives a correction built from two thin tables of 4,096 × 16 and 16 × 4,096 numbers, about 131,000 in total, so fewer than 1% of the numbers are trained ({fig:lora}).

@fig lora | figures/papers/lora_fig1.png | LoRA. The pretrained weight table W stays frozen; only the two thin tables A and B are trained, and their product is added to W. Here d is the size of the table and r the small inner size; A starts as small random numbers (written $\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$) and B as zeros, so training begins from the unchanged model. Reproduced from @{hu2021}, Fig. 1.

**Reinforcement learning (RL).** Let the model produce its own trajectories, score each with a **reward** (a number computed by a fixed rule, where higher is better) and adjust the weights so that high-reward trajectories become more likely. Because the model is scored on its own attempts, RL can reach strategies that no demonstration showed. We use **group relative policy optimization (GRPO)** [@shao2024]; the word "policy" simply means the model viewed as a rule for choosing what to output next. For each training video, GRPO samples a group of attempts (eight in this proposal), computes each attempt's reward and compares it with the group average. The comparison is scaled by the **standard deviation** of the group's rewards, a measure of how spread out they are (roughly, the typical distance of a reward from the average). An attempt above the average gets a positive **advantage** and is made more likely, one below gets a negative advantage and is made less likely, and one right at the average is left alone ({fig:grpo}). If all eight rewards are equal there is nothing to compare, every advantage is zero, and the group teaches nothing. An older method, PPO [@schulman2017], trains a second network to predict how good an attempt should be; GRPO replaces that network with the group average ({fig:grpo-paper}). A small penalty keeps the updated model from drifting too far from where it started.

@fig grpo | figures/fig_grpo.svg | GRPO in one picture, with illustrative rewards. An attempt's advantage is its reward minus the group average, divided by the standard deviation of the rewards within the group.
