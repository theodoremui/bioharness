### Why one camera is harder than three {#bg-cameras}

A photograph flattens the world. Every point along one line of sight from the camera lands on the same pixel, so the picture alone cannot say how far away something is ({fig:cameras}B). Several cameras at known positions solve this: each camera's line of sight through, say, the left ankle passes through a different set of places in the room, and the lines from different cameras cross where the ankle is ({fig:cameras}A). This is **triangulation**, the same geometry that lets two eyes judge distance.

@fig cameras | figures/fig_cameras.svg | Why depth is hard to recover from one camera. (A) With several cameras the lines of sight cross at one point, which fixes the 3D position. (B) With one camera, a small figure nearby and a large figure far away can produce the same image.

StaBLE was recorded with OpenCap [@uhlrich2023], which finds body landmarks in each phone's video, triangulates them into 3D and fits a model of the human skeleton to them. The result, which we call the **three-camera reconstruction**, gives joint angles and the path of the center of mass over time for every trial. This proposal treats it as the reference: the best available answer, against which everything measured from one camera is compared.

The practical goal is to get the same measurements from a single phone that anyone could prop up in a clinic or at home. OpenCap Monocular [@gilon2026] does this by fitting a learned 3D body model to one video and then correcting it with physical rules, for instance that a planted foot should not slide or sink through the floor ({fig:opencap-mono}). It replaces the missing geometry with knowledge learned from many other people. It also needs two facts that a single image cannot supply: the person's height, which fixes the size of the body in meters, and the phone's lens parameters, which relate pixel positions to viewing directions; OpenCap records both for each session, and the phone must stay still. It was validated on walking, squatting and standing up from a chair; how robust it is on less common movements, such as the long one-legged reach of the Y-Balance task, is less clear.
