### Testing fairly {#bg-fair}

A method is useful only if it works on people it has never seen, so the data are divided into a **development set**, used for every choice and every bit of training, and a held-back set used once at the end. The division is made by person and never by video. Each participant contributes up to 18 videos that share the same body, clothing and room; if one were used in training and another in testing, a model could match the person's appearance to their training videos and reproduce their typical score without measuring the movement. Any route by which information about the held-back people reaches the training process is called **leakage**, and it makes results look better than they will be in practice. Leakage can be subtle. StaBLE's rescaling endpoints $L$ and $U$ were computed from all 180 participants, so the published scale already contains information computed from the held-back people; this proposal therefore recomputes the endpoints from training people only.

Choices made during development are judged by **cross-validation**. The development people are split into five groups called **folds**; each candidate is trained on four folds and checked on the fifth, and the roles rotate so that every fold is checked once. Folds are **stratified**, meaning each fold receives a similar mix of balance scores, ages and elite athletes, so that no fold is unusually easy or hard. In practice, people are grouped by StaBLE score **quintile** (the score range cut into five bands that each hold a fifth of the people, also called ability quintiles), by age group and by elite-athlete status, and dealt out evenly. Each fold is then compared with the rest of the development set to catch large imbalances, using two standard checks: the **Kolmogorov–Smirnov test**, which asks whether two sets of numbers could plausibly come from the same distribution, and the **standardized mean difference**, the gap between two averages divided by the typical spread, which should stay below 0.1. A **percentile** is a position in sorted data: the 25th percentile is the value below which a quarter of the values fall.

### Measuring uncertainty {#bg-uncertainty}

A result measured on a few dozen people could have come out differently with other people. The **bootstrap** [@efron1979] estimates how differently. From the tested participants, draw the same number at random *with replacement*, so that some people are drawn twice and some not at all, keeping all of each person's tasks together; recompute the quantity of interest (for instance, the difference in rank agreement between two methods); and repeat 10,000 times ({fig:boot}A). The middle 95% of the results is a **95% confidence interval**. If it lies entirely above zero, the advantage is unlikely to be an accident of which people happened to be tested.

(B) Retraining with new random seeds shows how much a single training run can vary. Values are illustrative.

Training adds a second source of luck. Training involves random choices, such as the order of the examples and which attempts are sampled, and a **random seed** is the number that fixes those choices. Two runs of the same method with different seeds give different models ({fig:boot}B). A lucky run shifts every participant's result at once, so resampling people cannot average it away. This distinction separates two kinds of claim. A claim about *one delivered model* ("this agent orders the test people better") needs only the people resampled. A claim about *a training method* ("reinforcement learning improves this agent") must also allow for seed luck, which requires several seeds per method. Resampling the seeds and the participants together, each with replacement, is called a **crossed bootstrap**.

**Statistical power** is the chance that an experiment detects a real effect of a given size; small studies have low power and miss real effects. **Multiple comparisons** are a hazard because each test run at the 5% level has a 5% chance of a false alarm, so running many tests makes at least one false alarm likely; Holm's procedure [@holm1979] corrects for this by sorting the p-values (each test's probability of a result this extreme if there were no real effect) from smallest to largest and comparing them with progressively less strict thresholds ($0.05/m$ for the smallest of $m$ tests, then $0.05/(m-1)$, and so on, stopping at the first failure). Finally, every analysis is **pre-registered**: written down, with its decision rule, before the held-back data are opened, so that the rule cannot be adjusted to fit the result.
