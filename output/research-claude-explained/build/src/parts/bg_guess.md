### Why a low error can hide guessing {#bg-guess}

Mean absolute error rewards predictions that stay close to typical values, even when they say little about the individual. Suppose five people have true task scores 0.4, 0.5, 0.6, 0.7 and 0.8 ({fig:guess}). Guessing 0.6 for everyone misses by 0.2, 0.1, 0, 0.1 and 0.2, an average error of 0.120. A predictor that keeps the people in the right order but shrinks every difference to a fifth of its true size (0.56, 0.58, 0.60, 0.62, 0.64) has a lower error, 0.096, even though a person who balances far better than average receives a score only slightly above average. A noisy predictor that tracks each person (0.50, 0.45, 0.70, 0.60, 0.85) has an error of 0.080, similar to the shrunk one; it swaps two pairs of people but keeps the size of the differences between them. Error alone cannot tell these three apart, so we add three measures: rank agreement separates the constant guess, which orders no one, from the other two, and the calibration slope separates the shrunk predictor, whose differences are compressed fivefold, from the noisy one.

**Rank agreement** asks whether a method puts people in the right order. Within one task, replace the predicted scores by their ranks (smallest = 1) and do the same for the reference scores; the rank agreement is the correlation between the two rank lists, known as Spearman's correlation [@spearman1904]. It is 1 when the two orders are identical, about 0 when they are unrelated and −1 when one is the reverse of the other. With no tied values it can be computed as $1 - 6\sum d^2 / (n(n^2-1))$, where $d$ is each person's difference in rank and $n$ the number of people. For the noisy predictor the predicted ranks are 2, 1, 4, 3, 5 against true ranks 1 to 5, so $\sum d^2 = 4$ and the rank agreement is $1 - 24/120 = 0.8$. A constant guess gives every person the same rank, so there is no order to compare and rank agreement cannot be computed at all. Ordering people correctly is also how the StaBLE paper validated its score against clinical tests.

**Skill** compares a method's error with the constant guess's error on the same task: skill $= 1 - \text{error}/\text{error of constant guess}$. A skill of 0 means "no better than ignoring the video", 1 means perfect, and negative values mean worse than the guess. The shrunk predictor above has skill $1 - 0.096/0.120 = 0.2$.

**Calibration slope** is the slope of the best straight line through the points when predicted scores are plotted against reference scores. A slope near 1 means that when the true score rises by 0.1, the prediction rises by about 0.1 too; a slope near 0 means predictions barely move, collapsing toward the average. The shrunk predictor has slope 0.2 and the noisy one 0.85.
